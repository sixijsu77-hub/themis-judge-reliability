1. The null distribution of the slot-skew S, and the fixed threshold 0.05
==============================================================================
  50th percentile of S under uniform letters : 0.0400
  95th percentile of S under uniform letters : 0.0750
  99th percentile of S under uniform letters : 0.0900

  0.05 sits at the 69.1th percentile of that distribution.
  An unbiased judge exceeds it 30.9% of the time.
  A threshold inside the noise cannot separate no bias from some bias.

3. S against its own null: the second draft, and why it also cannot decide
==============================================================================
  letter distribution     true S  S <= null 95th   >= 4 of 5

  weak                     0.080          30.7%       3.4%

  null 95th percentile at this n = 0.0750
  Read on its own that separates the four rows. It is still the wrong rule,
  because S has a ceiling this design imposes and the ceiling depends on
  accuracy. The correct answer visits all four slots equally, so correct
  verdicts land on every letter equally and only errors can move S. The most
  hostile arrangement of a given error budget is computed by s_ceiling().

3c. The ceiling depends on the pattern of per-slot accuracy, not just its mean
==============================================================================
  obvious  mean acc    equal  one slot  measured
        3    0.9917   0.0062    0.0167    0.0083
        2    0.8117   0.1412    0.3767    0.3083
        1    0.6533   0.2600    0.5000    0.4850
        0    0.5200   0.3600    0.5000    0.5817

  Same mean accuracy, three arrangements of it, ceilings that differ by a factor
  of two or more. The figure quoted in the pre-registration is the 'measured'
  column -- conditional on the pilot's per-slot pattern, not a bound at that mean.
  The conclusion holds under all three: at obvious=3 every ceiling is under the null 95th percentile of 0.0750.

  H3 is asked at obvious=3. There the rule cannot fail: the same defect as the
  first draft with the sign reversed. A statistic diluted by 99% correct verdicts
  is the wrong place to look. Condition on the errors instead -- section 3b.

==============================================================================
3b. The rule that replaces it: conditional error share at A, null exactly 1/3
==============================================================================
  A first draft of this used the share over ALL wrong verdicts, with a null of
  0.25 = (3/4) x (1/3). That 3/4 assumes the error mass is spread evenly over the
  four arrangements, and on the pilot it is not -- see
  results/validation/decomposition.txt section 4, where the real null runs to
  0.31, above 0.25 and in the hypothesis's own direction. Dropping the
  arrangement whose answer is at A leaves a weighted average of three quantities
  that are each 1/3 under the null, so the null is 1/3 at any per-slot accuracy.

  n_err*   true 0.33   true 0.45   true 0.60   true 0.80
       5       4.8%       12.8%       33.2%       75.2% 
      20       2.9%       21.8%       70.6%       99.6% 
      40       2.2%       30.8%       91.8%      100.0% 
      47       2.6%       40.2%       96.5%      100.0% 
     120       2.1%       71.7%      100.0%      100.0% 

  First column is the false-positive rate; the rest are power. Below about 40
  usable errors nothing fires. 150 items at obvious=3 give the pilot judge 4,
  which is why H3 runs at all 1,763 items (about 47 for that judge): enough for a
  strong preference, not enough for a moderate one, and the pre-registration says
  so before the run. A judge more accurate than the pilot may not reach 40 even
  there, and is then reported as not evaluated rather than as passing.

4. Strict monotonicity of four noisy points, against a fitted slope
==============================================================================
  true first-slot rates    monotone   >=4 of 5  slope CI>0   >=4 of 5
  no effect                   3.6%      0.0%       3.1%      0.0%
  shallow                    62.3%     37.7%      93.8%     96.6%
  moderate                   97.7%     99.5%     100.0%    100.0%
  as piloted                100.0%    100.0%     100.0%    100.0%

  Both reject the flat world. On a shallow real trend the slope keeps it and
  monotonicity throws it away, which is the case P1 exists to resolve.
  Arrangements within an item are drawn correlated at the measured ICC {3: 0.0, 2: 0.0, 1: 0.0, 0: np.float64(0.12270026866462333)}, so the
  slope's interval is not assuming independence it does not have.

==============================================================================

  in use before this check:
     0  chosen     R1     R2     R3   chosen at A   distractors R1-R2-R3
     6      R1 chosen     R2     R3   chosen at B   distractors R1-R2-R3

    21      R3     R1     R2 chosen   chosen at D   distractors R3-R1-R2

  fixed order:
     0  chosen     R1     R2     R3   chosen at A   distractors R1-R2-R3
     6      R1 chosen     R2     R3   chosen at B   distractors R1-R2-R3
     8      R1     R2 chosen     R3   chosen at C   distractors R1-R2-R3
     9      R1     R2     R3 chosen   chosen at D   distractors R1-R2-R3

  Only [0, 6, 8, 9] moves the correct answer through every slot while leaving the
  three distractors in one order. Any other four confounds the two.
